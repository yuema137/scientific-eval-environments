# MMArch (2026)

> **English** | [简体中文](../zh/works/mmarch.md)

## Overview

MMArch is a 1,212-item multimodal benchmark built from figures in peer-reviewed architecture and civil-engineering papers, testing whether a model can gather visual evidence spread across a technical figure, identify the governing engineering principle, and apply it to reach a conclusion.

## Summary

MMArch targets a failure mode that figure-captioning and chart-QA benchmarks do not expose: an answer that requires combining several separated pieces of evidence inside one engineering figure and then reasoning about them with domain knowledge, rather than reading a value off the drawing. Items are generated by a deliberately decoupled pipeline in which a planner fixes a short reference answer before a writer composes a free-form question, so questions cannot be reverse-engineered from a pre-written response. Candidate items then pass automated screening, a blind adversarial audit, dual answer verification, and unanimous expert review. Eighteen open-weight and proprietary multimodal models are evaluated, and the best reaches roughly half of human expert accuracy.

## Tasks

1,212 short-answer items covering ten subdomains of architecture and civil engineering: structural engineering, seismic engineering, building physics, building inspection, heritage conservation, spatial analysis, building-envelope engineering, urban planning, BIM and digital construction, and computational design. Source figures are drawn from a pool of roughly 10,000 peer-reviewed papers from SCI-indexed journals and arXiv. Reference answers are spans of at most ten tokens; questions are free-form rather than templated. Construction proceeds through a decoupled planner–writer pipeline (the planner freezes the answer first), automated screening that rejects items solvable from text only, from the caption only, or that are too easy (eight trials per item), a blind adversarial audit by three agents that separately check evidence visibility, necessity of the governing principle, and shortcut solvability, dual-path answer cross-checking, and a final review by three professional architects and engineers who retain an item only by unanimous agreement. `TODO(reference)` — per-subdomain item counts are shown only as a figure and are not tabulated in the text.

## Domains

The benchmark spans architecture and **Civil & Structural Engineering**. Civil/structural membership is direct: structural engineering, seismic engineering, building inspection, and BIM and digital construction are four of the ten named subdomains, and the items require applying engineering principles to structural and building-system evidence. The remaining subdomains (heritage conservation, spatial analysis, urban planning, computational design, building physics, building-envelope engineering) are architectural or building-science topics without a separate canonical domain in this repository; no other science or engineering co-domain is assigned.

## Evaluation

Accuracy (%) against the frozen short reference answer, with normalisation for units, synonyms, numeric tolerance, and accepted answer sets. All models are decoded at temperature 0 with a maximum output length of 2,048 tokens. A human expert panel provides an upper reference. The paper also reports an error analysis assigning failures to five categories — perception, principle, composition, grounding, and consistency — and ablations showing that chain-of-thought and visual prompting give only small and inconsistent gains.

## Typical Duration

Single-turn multimodal question answering; the only stated budget is a 2,048-token maximum output per item. No trajectory length or wall-clock budget applies.

## Main Contribution

The authors position MMArch as the first benchmark that grounds multimodal reasoning in architectural and civil-engineering evidence, requiring models to compose distributed visual evidence with domain principles rather than recognise or extract from a drawing, and they document a large gap to human experts (roughly 52% for the best proprietary model against 94.57% for the expert panel).

## Key Design Ideas

- Answer-first, question-second generation: the planner freezes a ≤10-token answer before the writer composes the question, decoupling the two and reducing hallucinated question–answer pairs.
- Multi-stage filtering explicitly designed to remove text-only, caption-only, and shortcut-solvable items.
- A blind adversarial audit by three independent agents checking evidence visibility, principle necessity, and shortcut availability.
- Unanimous review by three practising architects and engineers as the final gate.
- Short constrained answers with unit/synonym/tolerance normalisation, so grading is automatic without an LLM judge.
- Failure taxonomy (perception, principle, composition, grounding, consistency) reported alongside accuracy.

## Strengths

- Items come from real peer-reviewed engineering figures rather than synthetic or textbook diagrams.
- The screening pipeline directly attacks the standard weakness of multimodal benchmarks — items answerable without looking at the image.
- Expert validation with unanimity and a human accuracy baseline of 94.57% establish that the items are solvable and the gap is a model gap.
- Grading is deterministic short-answer matching, so results are reproducible.

## Limitations

- Static short-answer question answering: no tool use, no simulator, no design or verification deliverable, so it does not measure engineering execution.
- Ten subdomains blend structural and civil engineering with architecture, urban planning, and heritage topics; the civil/structural share of the 1,212 items is not tabulated in the text.
- Ten-token reference answers bound the complexity of what can be asked and exclude multi-step design outputs.
- Repository note: sourcing figures from published papers of well-indexed venues carries a training-contamination risk that the paper's screening steps do not directly test for.
- Repository note: reported scores are tied to a 2026 model roster and will age faster than the item set.
