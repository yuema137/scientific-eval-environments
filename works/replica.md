# Replica (2026)

## Overview

Replica is a task space for paper replication in which an agent is given a research paper with one results figure irreversibly redacted and must reproduce that figure from the rest of the paper. Alongside it the authors release an auto-generated rubric-based judge calibrated against human raters, and use both to post-train Faraday, a 27B "AI Scientist" agent that calls coding agents as tools.

## Summary

The authors argue that replicating a paper is not a lookup task: the act of replication surfaces details the original left underspecified, so it demands the same hypothesis-driven exploration as open-ended research while still admitting a checkable target. Replica operationalises this by taking a published paper, selecting a main-text results plot, and removing it from the PDF, leaving the agent to reconstruct the experiment behind it. Task construction is itself a three-stage vision-language pipeline: a scan locates every main-text results plot and its caption, a localisation stage draws a bounding box inside an LLM-verifier repair loop, and the figure is then irreversibly redacted. Because the redaction is irreversible, the agent cannot recover the target by reading the source PDF. Scoring uses task-specific rubrics generated by an LLM with the gold plot hidden, so the rubric describes what a correct replication must establish rather than what the target figure looks like. The authors then post-train Faraday on this task space with a modified GRPO and compare it against frontier baselines on a held-out split.

## Tasks

**310 figure-replication tasks** — **242 training, 68 test** — drawn from **100 papers** in ML and AI-for-science published between **1990 and 2026**. Each paper contributes **1–13 tasks (median 2)**, and the set passes a manual quality filter. Task generation is powered by Gemini 2.5 Pro across the three vision-language stages described above. The test split separates in-distribution ML tasks from out-of-distribution AI-for-science tasks, so generalisation beyond the training distribution is measured directly rather than assumed.

## Domains

**AI & Machine Learning Research.** The source papers are ML and AI-for-science publications, and the replication target is a machine-learning results figure — training curves, ablations and benchmark comparisons — reproduced by re-running the underlying experiment. The AI-for-science subset draws on scientific applications of ML, but the paper does not report a per-field breakdown of that subset, so no science co-domain is assigned.

## Evaluation

Scoring is by an auto-generated **rubric-based judge**. Claude Opus 4.7 writes a task-specific rubric from a hand-designed meta-prompt with the gold plot withheld, spanning **five dimensions**: visual match, scientific claim support, experimental implementation, compute budget use, and scientific integrity.

Judge quality is reported against human raters (**n = 20** PhDs with ICML/ICLR/NeurIPS publications, **117 rankings**) using Kendall τ. The rubric judge agrees with humans at **τ = 0.19** against **τ = 0.15** for a baseline judge — but the more informative comparison is self-consistency: two independent rubric-judge draws agree at **τ = 0.66** versus **τ = 0.46** for the baseline judge, while **two humans agree at only τ = 0.30**. Three judge samples are drawn per rollout during training; eight-sample averaging brings noise down to the baseline judge's three-sample level.

On the held-out split, Faraday beats both Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on **73%** of in-distribution ML tasks and **60%** of out-of-distribution AI-for-science tasks, for a **6%** average improvement over Claude and **8%** over Codex. In a human preference study, raters preferred Faraday in **29 of 41** rollouts examined.

## Typical Duration

**60 minutes wall-clock** per task, on a **single one-seventh MIG slice of an H200 GPU**, with a **16K-token context window per turn**. Faraday itself runs a 128K-token context and invokes coding agents through a shell wrapper, able to launch several in parallel or reset their context.

## Main Contribution

A replication task space that is auto-generated rather than hand-curated — figure redaction turns any suitable paper into a task — paired with a rubric judge whose self-consistency exceeds inter-human agreement, and a demonstration that post-training a 27B model on that signal beats much larger frontier models on held-out replication.

## Key Design Ideas

- Irreversible figure redaction makes the target unrecoverable from the input, so the task cannot be solved by extraction from the source PDF.
- Rubrics are generated with the gold plot hidden, which keeps the judge from over-indexing on incidental visual properties of the target figure.
- The judge is validated on self-consistency as well as human agreement, and the paper reports that two humans agree less with each other (τ = 0.30) than two judge draws do (τ = 0.66).
- Faraday treats coding agents as tools rather than reimplementing coding ability, so the post-trained model learns research direction rather than code generation.
- A held-out AI-for-science split is kept distinct from the in-distribution ML split, so transfer out of the training distribution is reported separately.
- Compute is deliberately small and fixed per task (one MIG slice, 60 minutes), which bounds what any agent can attempt and keeps rollouts cheap enough to train on.

## Strengths

- The scoring target comes from a published paper the agent cannot see, so success is anchored to an external result rather than to the agent's own output or an unanchored judge.
- Task construction is automated end to end, which is what makes 310 tasks from 100 papers feasible and gives the space a clear route to growth.
- Judge noise is measured rather than assumed, and the paper reports the uncomfortable finding that its human raters agree with each other less than the judge agrees with itself.
- The 1990–2026 publication range spans far more than the recent-arXiv window most replication benchmarks draw on.
- Human preference is collected on actual rollouts by qualified raters, not by crowdworkers scoring summaries.
- The paper reports failures against its own thesis — cases where Faraday failed on papers the authors believe were rigorously obtained.

## Limitations

- No public release location is stated; the paper offers a contact email rather than a repository, so the task space is not currently reusable by others (recorded above as `TODO(reference)`).
- Agreement with humans is low in absolute terms (τ = 0.19). The authors' defence is that human-human agreement is itself low, which argues the ceiling is low rather than that the judge is good.
- The rubric judge is not validated on large-scale tasks, and the authors state that the human preference study does not support average-case conclusions.
- Every task is in silico under a 60-minute, one-MIG-slice budget, so results that need substantial compute or any physical experiment are out of reach by construction.
- The authors explicitly acknowledge construct-validity concerns for replication benchmarks generally.
- Repository note: the paper's headline contribution is Faraday, a post-trained agent. This card treats Replica and its judge as the reusable evaluation artifact; that emphasis is the repository's, and the training half of the work falls outside this repository's scope.
