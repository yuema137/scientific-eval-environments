## Month at a Glance

- Two works entered the knowledge base this month. Both are new releases, both are benchmarks, and there are no backfills: [ChemBench](../works/chembench.md), first public on 2024-04-01, and [OSWorld](../works/osworld.md), first public on 2024-04-11.
- The two sit at opposite ends of the evaluation spectrum and reach opposite verdicts on model capability. ChemBench measures chemical knowledge answered in a single turn and finds the best models beat the best human chemists in its cohort on average; OSWorld measures whether an agent can drive a real computer to a goal and finds the best model at 12.24% where humans clear over 72%.
- The gap between those two verdicts is not a claim about model quality. It is a consequence of what each benchmark measures — knowledge recalled in one turn versus a goal reached through many actions in a live environment.
- Each work also names its own boundary. ChemBench pairs its "superhuman" headline with overconfident answers and failures on some basic tasks, which the authors read as a safety concern rather than a clean win. OSWorld's human–model gap is widest on multi-application workflows — the tasks that require chaining actions across separate programs.

## What Changed This Month

**Two benchmarks answer "how capable are current models?" and disagree, because they measure different things.** The plain reading of a single headline number — "best models beat expert humans" or "best model reaches 12%" — invites a verdict on model capability in general. This month's two releases show why that reading fails: the number a benchmark reports is inseparable from the surface it measures on.

[ChemBench](../works/chembench.md) measures chemical knowledge and reasoning. It curates more than 2,700 question–answer pairs and scores state-of-the-art open- and closed-source models with an automated framework against a recruited cohort of human chemists. On that surface the best models outperform the best human chemists in the study on average. The measured quantity is what a model can recall and reason about in a single turn, with no tools and no laboratory or multi-step workflow. ChemBench is careful about the boundary of its own headline: the same models that score "superhuman" on average still stumble on some basic tasks and report their answers overconfidently, a combination the authors treat as both a capability result and a safety concern.

[OSWorld](../works/osworld.md) measures whether an agent can operate a real computer. It places a multimodal agent inside a live operating system — Ubuntu, Windows, or macOS — and scores 369 tasks by execution: a per-task script sets up an initial state, then evaluator functions check the actual artifacts the agent produced. On that surface humans complete over 72.36% of tasks while the best model, GPT-4 with accessibility-tree input, reaches only 12.24%. The measured quantity is not recalled knowledge but a goal state achieved across many steps in an environment the agent does not fully observe.

Read together, the month's evidence is that "how capable are today's models" has no answer independent of the evaluation surface. On a knowledge surface with no execution, frontier models already exceed expert humans on average; on an execution surface in a live environment, the same generation of models trails humans by roughly an order of magnitude. The two results do not contradict each other — they measure different competencies, and neither transfers to the other. What ChemBench leaves open is exactly what OSWorld probes: knowing chemistry says nothing about carrying a task to completion, and completing computer tasks says nothing about domain knowledge.

## Selected Topic Developments

### [Scientific Agent Benchmarks](../topics/scientific_agents.md)

[ChemBench](../works/chembench.md) is the primary reference for the static-knowledge end of this topic. It evaluates scientific capability as single-turn question answering rather than as a tool-using or laboratory workflow, and its distinguishing move is to make "superhuman" a measured comparison instead of rhetoric: a recruited chemist cohort supplies the human baseline, and automated scoring at a scale of 2,700+ pairs supports per-topic capability breakdowns rather than one aggregate figure. Probing confidence alongside accuracy is what surfaces overconfidence as a *distinct* failure mode — a model can be right on average and still miscalibrated about when it is wrong. The result stops at knowledge and reasoning: because nothing is executed and no tools are called, the score speaks to what a model knows, not to whether it could run a chemistry task end to end. The framework outlived the paper — the multimodal MaCBench runs on the same pipeline — which is why it reads as reusable infrastructure rather than a one-off leaderboard. The evaluated component here is [Scientific Problem Solving & Reasoning](../activities/scientific_problem_solving_reasoning.md).

### [General Long-Horizon Agent Benchmarks](../topics/long_horizon_evaluation.md)

[OSWorld](../works/osworld.md) is the primary reference for interactive, execution-evaluated benchmarking at operating-system scope. Where earlier interactive environments in this line are confined to the browser, OSWorld runs tasks inside whole operating systems and grades them by inspecting real state rather than matching an answer string. The mechanism is a per-task JSON configuration that drives four phases — initial-state setup from a VM snapshot, post-processing, getters that extract the artifacts to check, and evaluator functions that compare retrieved state against gold — backed by 134 hand-authored evaluation functions at roughly two hours of authoring per task. That execution grounding is what makes the reported 12.24%-versus-72.36% gap a statement about task completion rather than answer recall, and it locates the difficulty precisely: multi-application workflows, which require chaining actions across separate programs, are the hardest category at 2.97%. Two boundaries bound the claim. The reward is defined on [0, 1] to allow partial credit, but results are reported as a single mean success rate, so per-task partial achievement is not broken out; and the grade reflects only what the hand-written evaluator functions check.
