# July 2026 Monthly Report

> **English** | [简体中文](../zh/monthly/2026-07.md)

> **Coverage:** First appearances during 2026-07

## Month at a Glance

July 2026 is smaller than June by raw count, but more coherent. The month is dominated by one question: how do you evaluate agents in settings where the output has to answer to an external institution, physical process, or expert workflow rather than to a benchmark's internal scoring convention? The clearest examples are bridge inspection prioritization, physics literature review and proposal writing, structural reliability analysis, and world-model improvement under a fixed compute budget.

The second theme is that skill and trajectory work both become more adversarial. Instead of asking only whether a skill helps on average, July repeatedly asks whether a claimed skill use was causal, whether a lifecycle stage creates a security boundary, and whether intermediate signals can expose where long-horizon runs go off track.

## What Changed This Month

### [Scientific Agent Benchmarks](../topics/scientific_agents.md)

July's scientific benchmark work is unusually grounded in external reality. [Cognitive Agents for Bridge Inspection Prioritization](../works/cognitive-agents-for-bridge-inspection-prioritizat.md) is a good anchor because it does not score against a hand-authored label alone; it compares methods against observed future bridge deterioration across six annual NBI snapshots, and then checks the written rationales with a licensed inspector. The two physics-and-cosmology studies on literature review and proposal writing do something analogous from a different direction: they force AI outputs to answer to expert practice rather than to a synthetic benchmark protocol, and they explicitly measure fabrication, metadata mismatch, reviewer discrimination, and LLM-judge bias.

[Automating Structural Reliability Analysis with a Multi-Agent Large Language Model Framework](../works/automating-structural-reliability-analysis-with-a.md), [LQCDMaster](../works/lqcdmaster.md), [FukuyamaBench](../works/fukuyamabench.md), [Imaging-101](../works/imaging-101.md), [MDArena](../works/mdarena.md), [OntoLearner](../works/ontolearner.md), and [StructureClaw](../works/structureclaw.md) widen the disciplinary spread, but the more interesting addition is [AutoWorldModel-Bench](../works/autoworldmodel-bench.md). It sits inside scientific benchmarking because it evaluates iterative model improvement under a fixed budget, yet it also shows how far the repository's new scope reaches: evaluation is not only judging a finished agent, but structuring a controlled loop in which the next model update is itself the task.

### [Skill Hierarchy](../topics/skill_hierarchy.md)

July's skill work stops treating "skill use" as something the agent can simply claim in its own rationale. [BACKROOMBench](../works/backroombench.md) is the clearest statement of the problem: a skill-augmented agent may say it used a skill, but the only interesting question is whether intervention shows that the skill causally affected the answer. [SkillCoach](../works/skillcoach.md) extends that pressure into process evaluation by scoring selection, following, composition, and reflection separately while allowing the rubric to evolve from observed rollouts.

The second July shift is that skill repositories are being treated as attack surfaces and operational systems. [SkillSec-Eval](../works/skillsec-eval.md) decomposes the lifecycle into five trust boundaries; [LabRobFail](../works/labrobfail.md) evaluates failure analysis across control, physics, and semantic layers in self-driving laboratories; [SciExplore](../works/sciexplore.md), [SLBench](../works/slbench.md), and [UniClawBench](../works/uniclawbench.md) all contribute to the same broad message: once skills are reusable infrastructure, evaluation has to ask not only whether they help, but where they fail, how they can be manipulated, and whether the agent knows which level of the stack it is actually using.

### [Trajectory Evaluation](../topics/trajectory_evaluation.md)

Trajectory evaluation in July becomes more operational. [AgentLens](../works/agentlens.md) explicitly combines formal verification with LLM-written trajectory review and side-by-side comparison, which is a stronger claim than "we logged the steps." [Long-Horizon-Terminal-Bench](../works/long-horizon-terminal-bench.md) matters for a different reason: it introduces graded subtasks and dense intermediate credit, which turns a long terminal run into something the evaluator can inspect and the developer can act on. [OSReward](../works/osreward.md) and [Who&When Pro](../works/who-and-when-pro.md) continue the month's concern with intermediate responsibility assignment.

Taken together, the trajectory work in July is less about celebrating long traces and more about instrumenting them. The recurring move is to convert a black-box run into a sequence whose intermediate events can support review, blame, reward, or intervention.

## Selected Topic Developments

July keeps [Scientific Agent Benchmarks](../topics/scientific_agents.md), [Skill Hierarchy](../topics/skill_hierarchy.md), and [Trajectory Evaluation](../topics/trajectory_evaluation.md) active, but the interesting part is how they interact. Benchmarks are leaning on expert workflows and real outcomes, skill papers are becoming causal and security-aware, and trajectory work is building the instrumentation needed to connect those two sides.
