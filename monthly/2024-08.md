# August 2024 Monthly Report

> **English** | [简体中文](../zh/monthly/2024-08.md)

> **Coverage:** First appearances during 2024-08

## Month at a Glance

- Four works entered the knowledge base this month, and all four are new releases: [TransportBench](../works/transportbench.md) (first public 2024-08-15), [BLADE](../works/blade.md) (2024-08-19), [SimBench](../works/simbench.md) (2024-08-21), and [PEOA](../works/peoa.md) (2024-08-23).
- Every one is a field-specific scientific or engineering agent benchmark, and all four share a single topic — [Scientific Agent Benchmarks](../topics/scientific_agents.md) — yet no two target the same field: transportation engineering, data-driven science, multi-physics simulation, and chemical/process engineering.
- The shared question this month is not *which model wins* but *what an evaluator should grade*. The four disagree in practice: two grade a final textbook-style answer, one grades the analytical decisions behind an answer, and one grades a multi-turn simulator-building trajectory.
- The dividing line is whether the agent actually engages the scientific artifact. TransportBench and PEOA score static question answering; BLADE has agents interact with the data; SimBench has them build a running simulation over multiple turns.

## What Changed This Month

**Scientific agent evaluation fans out across four unrelated fields at once, and the interesting variation is in the evaluation target, not the leaderboard.** The prevailing shortcut for scoring a domain LLM is to collect subject-matter questions and check the final answer — cheap, but it certifies only that a plausible answer was produced, not that the reasoning or the analytical process was sound. August 2024's four additions all push against that shortcut from different directions, and reading them together separates two axes that a single benchmark usually conflates: *what is graded* and *whether the agent touches the artifact*.

**On the "what is graded" axis, the month spans from final-answer scoring to process scoring.** [TransportBench](../works/transportbench.md) keeps a final answer as the object but has human domain experts grade both the answer and the reasoning behind it across 140 undergraduate transportation-engineering problems, then adds two axes a leaderboard omits — answer consistency across repeated trials and stability under a "carefully check your solutions" follow-up. That extra scrutiny is what surfaces its headline result: accuracy and self-checking stability dissociate. Claude 3.5 Sonnet leads on accuracy (67.1%, 94/140) yet abandons the most previously-correct answers when asked to double-check (16 flipped, versus 4–6 for GPT-4 and GPT-4o), so a model's headline score does not predict its behavioral robustness. [PEOA](../works/peoa.md) goes further and decomposes scoring across the four stages of tool learning — planning, tool selection, tool calling, and response generation — so that a failure localizes to the step where a trajectory breaks rather than collapsing into one end-task number. [BLADE](../works/blade.md) drops the single answer entirely: because data-driven science admits several defensible analyses, it grades the *analytical decisions* an agent makes — which conceptual constructs, transformations, and statistical models it chooses — against independent analyses by expert data scientists, and reports on the diversity of those choices rather than one accuracy figure.

**On the "does the agent touch the artifact" axis, the month spans from text-only QA to interactive simulator construction.** TransportBench is explicit that it is a static text benchmark with no tool calls, code execution, or simulator in the loop, and it even replaces original figures with language descriptions so the test is pure language reasoning. PEOA's items are likewise textbook-style question–answer pairs, so it measures process-engineering *calculations* rather than executable process-systems work such as flowsheet construction or simulator-in-the-loop design. BLADE moves one step in: its finding is precisely that agents which *interact with the underlying data* show improved (though still non-optimal) diversity in their analytical choices versus base language models, making data engagement itself the measured variable. [SimBench](../works/simbench.md) sits at the far end — it asks 33+ simulator-oriented LLMs to stand up a working digital twin for the open-source Chrono multi-physics simulator through multi-turn dialogue, scored by an LLM judge under predefined rules with human-in-the-loop guidance, so the evaluated skill is iterative simulator-code generation rather than one-shot scripting.

**The month's cautions cluster around evaluation validity, and they are worth carrying forward rather than smoothing over.** PEOA reuses the same MathComp and ChemProc datasets to both instruction-tune the framework and evaluate it (a 70/15/15 split), so its close match to proprietary baselines compares an in-domain fine-tuned system against zero-shot models on the fine-tuning distribution, and its response-generation stage leans on surface-overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-L, Exact Match) that are weak proxies for numerical correctness. TransportBench is undergraduate-level and topic-imbalanced (public transit accounts for 55 of 140 problems), and its figure-to-text conversion removes the diagram interpretation intrinsic to geometric-design and network problems. SimBench's card is compiled from the arXiv abstract and official project materials, with numeric rankings still to be validated against the full paper. These are not disqualifying, but they bound what each result licenses.

## Selected Topic Developments

### [Scientific Agent Benchmarks](../topics/scientific_agents.md)

All four of this month's works are primary references here, and together they illustrate the topic's central premise: a scientific agent's output must be anchored to a domain-appropriate check, not to surface plausibility. Where they differ is in how far that anchoring goes, which is exactly the spread described above.

- [TransportBench](../works/transportbench.md) adds a field the topic did not previously cover — transportation systems engineering, taught inside a civil-engineering curriculum — and anchors correctness to human-expert grading of 140 course-derived problems rather than automatic string matching or an LLM judge. Its contribution to the topic is methodological as much as it is a new field: by measuring accuracy, repeated-trial consistency, and self-checking stability as three separate axes, it demonstrates a case where the accuracy ranking and the robustness ranking invert, which a single-number benchmark would hide. The boundary is that it is a static, single-turn, text-only test: no tool calls, code, or traffic simulator are in the loop, and the authors list tool-using agents as future work.

- [BLADE](../works/blade.md) targets data-driven science and moves the anchor from the answer to the analytical process. Because a research question over real data admits many defensible analyses, it grades the constructs, transformations, and statistical models an agent selects against independent expert analyses, and treats the *diversity* of defensible choices as a first-class quantity. Its reported result — that data-interacting agents improve on but do not reach expert-level diversity relative to base language models — is what makes it evidence for the topic's claim that engaging the artifact changes agent behavior. The boundary is scope: the paper's figure is 12 datasets (the repository lists more), and the evaluation covers analytical judgment on those datasets, not end-to-end scientific automation.

- [SimBench](../works/simbench.md) is the month's clearest interactive case: multi-turn digital-twin generation for the Chrono multi-physics simulator across five physics areas (multibody dynamics, finite element analysis, vehicle dynamics, robotic dynamics, and sensor simulation), scored by a rule-guided LLM judge with human oversight. It establishes multi-turn simulator-code construction — geometry, dynamics, and solver configuration bundled into one deliverable — as the evaluated skill, and is maintained by the Chrono developers so tasks stay aligned with the real simulator. Its boundary this month is validation: the card rests on the abstract and project materials, and the numeric model rankings await the full paper.

- [PEOA](../works/peoa.md) contributes a chemical- and process-engineering framework released with two datasets (MathComp, over 8,500 QA pairs; ChemProc, over 7,000 QA pairs) and a stage-decomposed evaluation protocol. Within this topic its distinctive move is that scoring is trajectory-aware rather than end-answer-only — the same property that places it on two adjacent axes:
  - On [Trajectory Evaluation](../topics/trajectory_evaluation.md), the four-stage metric family (planning, tool selection, tool calling, response generation) scores intermediate tool-use decisions against gold plans and ground-truth tool sets, localizing where along a trajectory a solution breaks instead of reporting one number.
  - On [Skill Hierarchy](../topics/skill_hierarchy.md), the meta-agent-over-experts design — a coordinator that decomposes a query into sub-tasks and routes each to an instruction-tuned small-scale specialist model — is a concrete instance of decomposing a complex task across a hierarchy of narrower skills.
  
  PEOA's boundaries are described in full under *What Changed This Month*: shared fine-tune/evaluate data, surface-overlap generation metrics, and textbook-style items that test calculations rather than executable process-systems tasks.
